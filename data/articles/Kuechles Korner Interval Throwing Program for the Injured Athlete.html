By David Kuechle, M.D.

Intro

This post describes a program designed to return an athlete back to throwing following an injury or surgery.  As such, if you are here to read about an off-season throwing program designed to turn your noodle arm into a massive, fire-breathing cannon of baseball manliness, you are out of luck. Throwing is a highly demanding activity. For a throwing athlete attempting to return from injury, getting back to throwing can be difficult. If the athlete attempts to progress too quickly, recovery can be slowed or worse, recurrent or more severe injury can result. In this post I will try to synthesize from available research the basics of an interval throwing program (ITP) designed to return athletes to throwing in a safe, structured way. I borrowed heavily from work done by UF Orthopedic and Sports Medicine Institute (Go Gators!) The essential goals of the program are to restore motion, strength, endurance and confidence to the throwing arm. This is done through throwing a set number of reps at graduated distances during the flat ground program. There is also a mound program for pitchers.

Caveats

In order to begin an ITP, the athlete needs to be cleared by his physician and/or physical therapist. From my standpoint, this usually means the player has full motion, strength that is adequate for throwing as determined by the physical therapist and no pain. In most cases, the athlete in question has generally completed a course of physical therapy and has been cleared by their therapist to begin throwing.

Prior to starting to throw, a proper warm-up is required to reduce risk of further injury. There are various ways to do this. I wrote a blog post on this very topic for the PSSBL website a number of years ago. At the time, I made some YouTube videos with my pals from Experience Momentum and they still exist! One is entitled “15 minute baseball dynamic warmup” and the other is “5 minute baseball warmup”.  I would advise starting with the 15 minute version before a throwing session and using the 5 minute version in between throwing sets that call for an additional short warm-up. After completing a throwing session, I recommend an active cool-down comprising of light aerobic activity, rubber band rotator cuff and upper back resistance exercises and stretching, followed by heat or ice.

In terms of progression, time tables vary depending on the individual athlete and the severity of the injury. The key is to complete the step without pain or significant fatigue. This requires that you be honest with yourself! Each step must be completed twice without pain before progressing to the next step. Sharp pain in the affected joint is an automatic stopping point for that session. If the symptoms do not resolve after a couple of days of rest, TLC and contemplation of potential flaws in your throwing mechanics, reevaluation of the injured joint by your friendly orthopedic surgeon is strongly advised. Even while progressing normally, 1 day of rest is required between throwing sessions – or more if there is lingering soreness. You can’t safely rush this program. Sore and fatigued muscles are prone to injury. Throwing sessions may resume once the symptoms clear.

When performing the flat ground throwing program, each throw should be performed using a crow-hop in order to engage the entire body. This is very important as it helps to reduce injury. The throwing program should be performed every other day with a day of rest in between (for instance Monday, Wednesday and Friday) with a maximum of 3 throwing sessions per week. Any time a step is painful, stay at that step or go back to a prior step until you can progress pain-free. Last, in terms of intensity, when throwing for a set distance, you want the ball to get to your throwing partner without an excessive loop – but you are not playing “burn-out” either. The ball just needs to have enough momentum to hit your target comfortably.

The Program

The throwing program is split into two parts: flat ground and mound throwing. All athletes need to complete the flat ground portion before progressing to playing. Pitchers also need to complete the mound portion. It should be noted that the flat ground section ends at 120’. Outfielders (who are generally made of tougher stuff than regular baseball players) may want to extend the sessions to 150’ and possibly even 180’ before returning to play, depending on their level of confidence. Again, use the 15 min dynamic warm-up as the beginning of each throwing session.

Mound Program

For pitchers, the next phase involves a graduated ramp-up of the number of throws and intensity while throwing off of a mound. Each phase starts with crow-hop throws using Step 9 from the basic program above (minus the cool-down) as the warm-up, before getting on the bump and using the normal pitching motion. This is the time to really be mindful of your mechanics and try to incorporate your lower body and core into each pitch to reduce arm stress. Initially the pitcher throws only fastballs, before adding breaking pitches towards the end of the program. Perceived intensity is obviously fairly subjective – unless you have access to a Trackman. Err on the side of caution and as always, ease up or stop if there is pain.

At this point, if all phases have been completed at least twice without significant fatigue or pain, the pitcher can return to play with a pitch count. That doesn’t mean only throwing 150 pitches instead of the usual 234 (looks at Carl). I would start with around 30 pitches and then increase by 15 or so per outing.

Summary

So there you have it – a relatively straightforward progression to help reduce some of the uncertainty when returning to play after injury. The success of the program hinges on the athlete being honest about pain. Some discomfort is expected as the throwing intensity ramps up, but it should be short-lived and fairly mild. Sharp pain is not acceptable. Progress as your body allows and try not to get frustrated if soreness slows you down a bit.
