Many PSSBL players either coach youth baseball or have children who play. Arm injuries in particular are common in young athletes, even more so since year around leagues became commonplace. Some youths participate on more than one team. Young athletes are encouraged to focus on a single sport and even a single position within a given sport at increasingly younger ages. In my clinic, I see many young players with arm problems. The types of injuries vary from soft tissue trauma to tendons and ligaments, to skeletal injuries in growing bones. Activities and behaviors have been identified that increase the risk of injury in young athletes. Fortunately, preventative measures exist that can be utilized to reduce this risk.

The vast majority of injuries are due to overuse. There are guidelines for the recommended number of pitches per outing based on age, the types of pitches thrown, the number of rest days after throwing which I will cite in a bit. Some injuries are due to pitching through pain, others are due to improper mechanics, or physical limitations (tight shoulder girdle, tight hamstrings, weak core muscles) and improper or inadequate warmup.

Overuse or repetitive trauma is the number one cause of arm injuries that I see in my clinic. Having the athlete play more than one position, and not pitch for more than one team are two straightforward ways to lower the stress on a growing athlete’s arm. In addition, Little league baseball has come out with recommendations regarding the number of pitches a young athlete should throw in a single outing.

After throwing, reducing inflammation with ice (20 minutes on/20 minutes off) is useful. A couple of doses of Tylenol or an anti-inflammatory like ibuprofen or naproxen can help relieve pain (more on medications later).

In general, try not to emphasize how hard a young pitcher can throw; instead emphasize mechanics and command. Never use a radar gun, which leads to madness (and over-throwing). It’s also useful to be aware that certain types of pitches put increased strain on a growing athlete’s arm. Here are some recommendations for when a certain type of pitch should be introduced.

There are certain physical limitations that can predispose an athlete to injury. There was a study done some years ago at the famed Kerlan-Jobe clinic (home of the Tommy John surgery) that looked at which muscles were activated during throwing in amateur versus professional pitchers. In a nutshell, the amateur pitchers threw primarily with their smaller arm muscles, while the professional pitchers tended to use their larger muscles like latissimus, pectoralis and deltoid more. If you’ve ever wondered why that skinny guy on your high school baseball team could throw harder than you, he was probably better able to recruit his larger muscles than you. Either that, or you have a noodle arm. The Kerlan-Jobe study looked only at upper extremity muscles, but in reality, we throw using our whole body. Our core abdominal and lower extremity muscles are critical to throwing effectively and many injuries result from trying to throw with deficiencies in some of these muscle groups. Weak, tight or improperly warmed up core and lower body muscles lead to reliance on smaller, more vulnerable arm muscles to throw, predisposing a throwing athlete to injury. Training and physical therapy can help address some of these deficiencies.

Throwing efficiently also requires the appropriate mechanics. Learning to utilize the entire body to throw will reduce strain on the arm and improve performance. It’s not always easy to know how to teach your young throwers how to do this. Fortunately, in our league we have some solid resources. To mention a couple, on the east side there is Fernando DaSilva with Dragon’s baseball, and on the west side there is Ryan Patrick. So, if you have questions regarding pitching mechanics, make use of the knowledge we have available to us.

If you’ve done everything right for your young pitcher and yet they continue to complain of a sore arm, there might be a structural injury or physical limitation that needs to be addressed by a medical professional. Have an honest dialogue regarding pain. Don’t ask the player to pitch through it. Get them checked out by your friendly orthopedic surgeon. Many injuries can be treated with rest, followed by physical therapy and a progressive throwing program. Sometimes, though fortunately rarely, surgery is required. If you’re concerned, it’s generally better to have a sore arm looked at sooner rather than later.

I’ll end with a few words about anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs). In kids, I have to say I’m not a big fan of their use for more than a few doses. NSAIDs essentially have two functions: reduce inflammation and relieve pain. However, to get the anti-inflammatory benefits, NSAIDs have to be in the blood stream at a certain level for at least several days. The way most of us use ibuprofen or naproxen (two of the most common over the counter anti-inflammatories) is episodic - taking the medication when we’re sore, often at doses far exceeding the recommended maximum. So, we’re not really getting the anti-inflammatory benefits, just the temporary pain relief. In addition, use of NSAIDs can have detrimental side effects, causing stomach upset or even ulcers, damaging kidneys or liver, elevating blood pressure and more. In children, NSAIDs can be used sparingly for pain relief (or use Tylenol, a safer medicine in my opinion), but I wouldn’t recommend regular, ongoing use of them. If your athlete has a sore arm after a day or two, something might be structurally wrong that requires medical evaluation and treatment. Masking pain with medication can allow for further damage. If you’re concerned, have them checked out!
