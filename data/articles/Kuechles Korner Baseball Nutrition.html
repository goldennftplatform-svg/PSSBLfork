There is a lot of conflicting information out there about nutrition and weight loss. I asked my friend Shanon Tysland at Experience Momentum for some insight on nutrition and he directed me to Nicole Thelan, RDN who is the nutrition department manager there. This is what she had to say:

 

Eating Enough and Prioritizing Balance  

Let’s start with the basics: Eating enough to meet your body’s needs is key to your success, both as an athlete and as a human. With all the different diets discussed in the media, it might feel tempting to try one out. As registered dietitians, we caution against fad diets because they can challenge the body‘s natural metabolism and energy systems, leaving you feeling fatigued and increasing the risk for injury. To keep it simple, it’s best to aim to eat breakfast within 2 hours of waking up and continue to provide the body with food every 3 -5 hours throughout your day. Think of your metabolism like a fire you might build while camping. If you wait too long to put in wood, the fire goes out and you must put in larger amounts of wood to get the fire going again. Alternatively, if you continuously add sticks and logs, the result is a robust fire. Your body works similarly; rather than waiting long periods of time to fuel up, we should fuel continually throughout the day to set ourselves up to have the energy needed for practice or games.  

 

Now, you might be wondering, “well what should I be eating every 3 – 5 hours”? Generally, meals should include sources of protein, fat and carbohydrates as well as a pop of color in the form of fruit and/or vegetables. When you pair carbohydrate foods with sources of protein and fat, you promote blood sugar balance and are less likely to experience any unpleasant blood sugar highs or lows. Breakfast is a particularly important meal, as it kick-starts your metabolism and sets you up for the rest of the day. Athletes who consume breakfast daily are more likely to have increased energy and better focus, translating to enhanced performance. Ensuring you eat regularly throughout the day also means you’re less likely to experience cravings that can lead to eating past fullness later in the day. So, the adage that “breakfast is the most important meal of the day” is true, especially for anyone engaging in regular physical activity. Snacking is a great way to help fill those gaps in our day, but it often gets a bad rap, in part because they might not be as well planned as a meal. A good rule of thumb for snacks is the carb buddy system – unless you are having a pre-exercise snack, we recommend that foods that provide mainly carbohydrates have a “buddy” in the form of a source of protein or fat. Why? Because carbohydrates spike your blood sugar. This is desirable before a game or practice because your body will use that energy as fuel for the activity. However, when going about your normal life or while working, those blood sugar spikes can lead to crashes impacting energy and focus. Foods that provide protein and fat digest slowly, cushioning the release of glucose from carb-based foods. Additionally, protein and fat are satiating and you’re more likely to feel full for longer if you top your toast with nut butter or pair your crackers with cheese.  

 

Importance of Recovery Nutrition Plan 

Another crucial factor for the aging athlete is recovery nutrition. Regardless of your age, it’s important to re-fuel after any activity that lasts 45 minutes or longer. Recovery nutrition promotes muscle growth, refuels your energy stores and decreases your chance of injury. However, as we age protein intake after exercise becomes more critical because the processes our body uses to restore energy and rebuild muscle become less efficient. To help mitigate this, follow the 3 R’s of recovery – refuel your body with carbohydrates, repair your muscles with protein and rehydrate with fluid and electrolytes. Aim to eat a post activity meal or snack within 30 – 45 minutes that provides you with at least 20 grams of protein. Examples of foods that provide about 20 grams of protein include 1 can of tuna or salmon, 3 large eggs, 3 ounces of chicken or turkey, or 6 ounces of Greek yogurt with a granola that includes nuts or seeds. You should also consume carbohydrates in a post-exercise meal to help refill the stored energy used and rebuild your muscles. Ideally, your post workout meal should include at least twice as much carbohydrate as protein because carbohydrates help facilitate muscle growth when paired with protein. So, adding two slices of bread to a turkey sandwich, pairing the Greek yogurt with a cup of fruit or stir frying a cup of rice with your chicken provides the macronutrients necessary for recovery.   

 

Lastly, never forget to rehydrate. As we age it becomes more important to focus on staying hydrated to help the systems in the body run efficiently. For most adults, aiming for at least 60 ounces of water daily plus an additional 20 ounces for every hour of activity helps meet hydration goals. After exercise, and especially if practicing outdoors, the addition electrolytes will allow your body to absorb more fluid and replace electrolytes lost through sweat. Products like Nuun, Liquid IV and Gatorade are convenient and can provide the amount of sodium, chloride, potassium, magnesium and calcium needed for replenishment of your electrolytes.  

 

Nicole Thelan, MS, RDN

Thanks to Nicole, we now have some information about what we should be eating. What strategies can we use to help drop some weight? Portion size is obviously important. Nicole introduced the idea of snacking periodically throughout the day to avoid becoming ravenous and eating 12 pounds of spaghetti at dinner. Something as simple as eating off a salad plate can fool your brain into thinking you have a more robust portion size than you actually do. Dishing up your plate in the kitchen and then bringing it to the table can curb the impulse to have seconds (and thirds). Taking your time as you eat to enjoy your food allows your brain’s satiation centers to register your stomach’s degree of fullness. Pushing away from the table when you’re satisfied but not uncomfortably full seems self-evident but can be difficult for some of us, myself included. Cutting back on alcohol, added sugar and processed foods is also important.

Of course, regular exercise is another critical element of weight loss. Calories in versus calories burned is a useful concept to consider. We lose about 1% of our lean body mass each year after age 40. This is reversible with resistance training. So, how much exercise is enough? The World Health Organization has recommended at least 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous physical activity per week for healthy adults age 18-64. Aerobic exercise should be performed for at least 10 minutes at a time – so, roughly the time Carl’s catchers spend chasing balls he’s thrown to the backstop during a bullpen. In addition, 2 episodes of resistance training for 30 minutes each is also recommended. This. Of course, is a guideline. For people with specific health conditions that preclude this level of activity, adjustments can be made to these recommendations to accommodate limitations.

So now you have all the tools you need: guidelines on proper nutrition, strategies for reducing your food intake and recommendations on weekly exercise goals. Incorporate these into your life and your teammates will be calling you “Snakehips” before you know it.
