If you knew Craig Hogan, you remember his organizational skills, his calmness, his competitiveness, his custodial care of the fields we played on. Maybe you even recall his mysteriously effective, gravity-defying cut "fastball."

But my field time with Craig—four years when he was my manager in the fledgling Rainier Division, and three years when I was his manager in the Sierra Division—shine a light on a Craig Hogan not everyone had the privilege of knowing.

Generosity

One dreary, overcast afternoon, I drove a rental car to a game we played at BCC. As a rule, driving was not something I did, since I chose to live without owning a car. Our game completed, I walked the little hill to the parking lot, turned the ignition key to my rental, and . . . nothing. I tried a few more times before the only other player left in the lot came over.

“Good ball game, Dave.” It was Craig. “But are we having a problem here?” “For some reason, my car won’t start.”

He glanced in at the dashboard. “It looks like your lights have been on the whole game. Battery’s probably dead. Say, do you have jumper cables?”

“I doubt it. This is a rental.”

We checked the trunk, empty except for my gear.

Craig retrieved his phone from his car. “I’ll tell you what. I’m a member, and if I can get the AAA folks on the line, I’ll have them come out and give you a jump.”

Craig waited with me for the half hour it took for AAA to arrive and give me a jump, killing time in that parking lot at BCC like it was what he had planned to do with his evening all along.

***

Craig managed our team the only year I played in the MSBL World Series in Phoenix. My experience there concluded the day after we lost our semi-final playoff game. The night before, my roommate borrowed two hundred dollars from me to help pay for his portion of our Mesa Sheraton Hotel bill. He had brought cash, but not enough, and didn’t have a credit card. He promised to have the money for me when I got back to Seattle. “Just contact me,” he said.

I returned to Seattle, and over several weeks my attempts to contact my roommate were fruitless. I had mentioned the issue to Craig, and when we met as a team in early Spring the next season, he asked if I had ever received my money.

“No. I never did.”

“Well let me look into it and see what I can do.”

At our next practice Craig handed me his own personal check for $200. “I’m sorry that happened, Dave. But this should take care of it.”

Enthusiasm

Craig bristled with his desire to play ball. The first two years of our Rainier Division 1917 White Sox team, Craig scheduled practice games often, sometimes with teams that were not part of the PSSBL, and sometimes even after the regular season had begun. Who we played didn’t matter, as long as they could field a competitive team. We traveled as far south as Tacoma and north to Stanwood, just to play a game of baseball.

Craig’s enthusiasm produced memorable lines.

“Fellahs, at our age, whether you’re playing ball or not, simply standing up for three hours is an accomplishment.”

“Regardless of how our ball game turns out, this beats staying home and cleaning the gutters.”

At one night game, as guys groaned and sighed through pre-game warmups, Craig took on an ecclesiastical tone. “Fellahs, I like to think the Bible has Senior Baseball in mind when it says, The Spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak.”

Good Taste

In the first year of the 1917 White Sox, at the end of some games, as players were changing out of our cleats, outfielder Bob Matthews would look pleadingly to his left and right at teammates in the dugout and say, “Anybody want to go out for a soda pop?” A beer is what Bob had in mind, and finally, after a mid-season game at old Husky Stadium, a half dozen or more guys said sure, let’s go.

Orders for Bud Lite or Rainier were placed, and then the waitress turned to Craig.

“And for you?”

“I’ll have the darkest ale you’ve got.”

The Bud Light and Rainier drinkers stopped talking and stared at Craig as though he had just given his hitter the take sign on an 0 - 2 pitch.

“What’s the matter? You guys never drank a beer that actually had some taste?”

***

In my phone conversation with him several weeks before he passed away, Craig talked about how fortunate he was to have participated in two activities as an adult that meant so much to him, and how both flying and baseball put him in touch with people with whom he had little else in common, but with whom he had become good friends. That’s just how I feel about having been blessed with the opportunity to meet on the ball field with Craig Hogan, a great human whose memory will live with me as the innings of life roll on.

David Mahler
